## How much sleep do runners need
Most adults need 7-9 hours. Athletes in hard training often do better near the top of that range. Short sleep raises perceived effort, slows recovery and increases injury and illness risk. Consistent timing matters as well as the total.

## Shifting to an earlier wake-up
To prepare for a 5 AM race start, shift bedtime and wake time earlier by about 15-30 minutes every few days rather than all at once. Get bright light soon after waking, keep lights dim in the evening, and keep the same times on weekends as far as you can.

## Naps
A 20-30 minute nap before mid-afternoon can offset short sleep, for example after an early long run. Longer or later naps can make night sleep worse.

## Sleep the night before the race
Nerves often ruin the last night. This is normal and usually has little effect if the previous two nights were good. Prioritise sleep two nights before the race, go to bed early the night before, and rest even if you cannot fall asleep.

## Evening habits for better sleep
Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet, avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening, avoid heavy meals and alcohol close to bed, and dim screens for the last 30-60 minutes. Finish eating about two hours before bed.

## Signs you are not recovering
Warning signs include higher RPE for the same pace, heavy legs that do not clear after 48-72 hours, poor sleep, low motivation, mood changes, frequent colds and ongoing soreness. When several appear together, cut intensity or take extra rest days and review sleep and food.

## Normal muscle soreness versus pain
General dull muscle soreness 24-72 hours after harder training is normal. Sharp, localised, worsening or one-sided pain, swelling, pain that changes your stride, or pain at rest is not normal. Stop running and get advice, because running through it often turns a small problem into weeks off.

## Illness rule of thumb
If symptoms are only above the neck (a mild runny nose), an easy short run is usually fine. If you have fever, chest symptoms, body aches or feel very unwell, rest until you are well, then return gradually.

## Strength and mobility
One or two short strength sessions a week for calves, glutes, hamstrings and core help running economy and injury resilience. Keep them light in the last week before a long run and the race. Mobility work on rest days is useful but optional.

## Shoes and kit
Use running shoes that are comfortable and have been worn for a few runs, and do not wear brand-new shoes on race day. Test socks, clothing and anti-chafe products on long runs to prevent blisters and rubbing.

## Training in heat and humidity
Heat raises effort and sweat loss. Run in the cooler early morning or evening, slow down by effort, drink and use electrolytes, wear light clothing and sun protection, and allow about 10-14 days to adapt. Stop and seek help for dizziness, confusion, chills in the heat, stopping sweating or nausea.

## When to see a doctor
Seek medical advice for chest pain or pressure, fainting, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, severe or lasting pain, injuries that do not improve in a week or two, or if you have a medical condition or take medication and are increasing training. This app is a training tool and not a substitute for medical care.

## Weekly mileage and sudden increases
Injury risk rises when load jumps quickly. If a week ends up much bigger than the one before, follow it with an easier week. Smooth, gradual progress beats heroic weeks.
