## BMR versus daily energy needs
Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the energy used at complete rest. It is not your daily calorie need. Total daily energy expenditure adds non-exercise activity (job, walking, daily movement) and the cost of training. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation estimates BMR; the activity multiplier and run cost are estimates, so treat any calorie target as a starting point and adjust from weight trend and performance.

## A sensible deficit for a runner
For fat loss while training, a modest deficit of roughly 200-300 kcal per day is usually enough and protects performance, recovery and mood. Aim for about 0.25-0.5% of body weight lost per week at most. Bigger deficits during a hard training block raise the risk of fatigue, illness, injury and lost muscle.

## When to pause the deficit
Eat at maintenance on long-run days, during race week and the taper, and whenever recovery is poor: persistent fatigue, falling motivation, worse sleep, higher-than-usual RPE at the same pace, frequent illness or niggling injuries. Fat loss can resume after the race.

## Protein for endurance athletes in a deficit
A common range is about 1.6-2.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day. Spread it across meals, roughly 0.3 g/kg (about 20-40 g) per meal, which supports muscle repair and helps keep lean mass while losing fat.

## Carbohydrate matches training load
Carbohydrate is the main fuel for running. Rough daily ranges: about 3-5 g/kg on rest or light days, 5-7 g/kg for around an hour of moderate training, and 6-10 g/kg for very high-volume days or the day before a long race. Eat more carbohydrate on long and quality days, less on rest days.

## Fat and fibre
Keep dietary fat adequate, around 20-35% of energy and not below about 0.8-1 g/kg, for hormone health and absorption of vitamins. Fibre is good for health (about 25-35 g/day) but reduce high-fibre foods in the meal before long runs and on race morning to avoid stomach trouble.

## Weight fluctuates daily
Body weight can swing 1-2 kg day to day from water, glycogen, salt, food in the gut and the menstrual cycle in women. After a long run or a big carbohydrate day, weight often rises. Judge progress from a 7-14 day trend rather than single weigh-ins.

## Warning signs of under-fuelling (low energy availability)
Persistent tiredness, frequent illness, injuries that will not settle, poor sleep, irritability, feeling cold, loss of libido and slow recovery suggest you are not eating enough for your training. Reduce or stop the deficit and speak to a doctor or sports dietitian if signs continue.

## Pre-run meal
Before runs, eat familiar, mostly carbohydrate food that is low in fat and fibre. For runs over about an hour, a meal of about 1-2 g/kg carbohydrate 2-3 hours before works well; closer to the run, a small snack of about 30-60 g carbohydrate 30-60 minutes before is enough. For an early-morning run, a banana and toast with honey is a typical choice. Practise in training.

## Post-run meal
After runs, eat carbohydrate and protein, for example about 1 g/kg carbohydrate and 20-40 g protein, within an hour or two. It matters most when you train again within 8 hours or after long and hard runs. After a short easy run a normal meal is enough.

## Caffeine
Moderate caffeine, about 3-6 mg/kg taken around an hour before, can improve endurance performance. Test it in training, avoid it late in the day because it harms sleep, and skip it if it upsets your stomach or you are not used to it.

## Supplements
Most runners do not need supplements. Iron, vitamin D, and similar are only worth taking if a blood test shows a need, and should be guided by a doctor. Be wary of fat burners and extreme products.
