source: General endurance-training principles (Pfitzinger/Douglas, Daniels, Hal Higdon-style novice plans, ACSM), summarised in my own words

## Purpose of the long run
The long run builds endurance, fat-burning efficiency, durability of muscles and tendons, and mental confidence. It is run at easy effort. Time on feet matters more than speed.

## How big should the longest run be before a half marathon
Many half-marathon plans peak between about 16 and 19 km. You do not need to run the full 21.1 km in training. Going much beyond what you can recover from adds injury risk with little extra benefit.

## Progression rule of thumb
Increase the long run by roughly 1-2 km at a time, and total weekly distance by no more than about 10% on average. The 10% figure is a guide, not a law. Bigger jumps, especially in the last weeks before a race, raise injury risk.

## Long-run pace
Run long runs at an easy conversational effort, the same as or slightly slower than normal easy pace. If your pace drifts faster over the run, slow down. Walking for a minute at drink stops is fine.

## Cutback and taper weeks
Reducing distance for a week every third or fourth week helps absorption of training. Before a race the last long run is usually about two weeks out, followed by a taper.

## Missed sessions
If you miss a run, do not squeeze the missed distance into other days. Resume the plan where it is. After one missed easy run nothing changes. If you miss many runs in a row, shorten the next long run rather than jumping back to the planned distance.

## Why Saturday should be short and easy before a Sunday long run
Hard or long runs the day before the long run leave tired legs, which makes the key session worse and raises injury risk. Saturday should be a short easy run or rest. Four runs per week is a maximum, not a requirement; skipping Saturday is fine when recovery is poor.

## Weekly structure for four running days
A sensible mix is two weekday runs (one easy, one with some quality), a short easy run and one long run. The other days are rest, mobility or easy cross-training. Hard days should not be back-to-back.

## When to shorten or skip a run
Shorten or skip when you are ill with symptoms below the neck or a fever, when you have sharp or worsening pain, after very poor sleep combined with heavy fatigue, or when soreness stays high for days. Missing one run costs far less than an injury.
